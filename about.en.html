First projects

Learning not just in lectures

During our studies we created a student research society practically from scratch. We had to organise work, align people, and find resources for development. We received a grant of 36,000 rubles for this.

It was one of the first times we saw an idea turn into a concrete undertaking: with tasks, deadlines, responsibility, and a result.

Our first business

CyberKids

In third year we opened CyberKids with a friend — an offline project related to teaching computer skills.

The friend proposed the idea and handled curriculum. Adelia was responsible for design, I — for legal, financial, and organisational matters.

In practice, we did everything together on site: drove for equipment, assembled workstations, filed paperwork, solved daily tasks. We opened in the morning so visitors could sign up or print documents. We paid ourselves no salary — money went to rent.

In autumn 2024 we closed the offline point. This experience didn't turn into a ready-made business model we'd want to scale, but it helped us understand the economics of a small venture. We saw firsthand how much fixed costs affect a project and why simply organising work well isn't enough.

Less fixed costs — more room to experiment

After the offline business we started paying closer attention to how we launch new projects.

If a product can be created and tested without an office, equipment, or other significant upfront costs, that gives more freedom. You can validate an idea faster, understand whether users need it, and avoid investing too much in something that still exists only in our imagination.

This approach turned out to be especially close to us in IT product development.

From ideas — to apps

We continued working on our own projects and participating in tech entrepreneurship support programs.

One of the main results was AdiGuide — an app with audio guides for self-guided city walks. After several unsuccessful attempts to get support, we won the "Student Startup" grant of 1 million rubles to develop the project.

For us this became an opportunity to move from working on an idea to more systematic product development.

Over time other projects appeared: Forseta, ToDooliCo, CarMind. They solve different problems and are at different stages of development, but what unites them is one thing: we want to develop our own products, not limit ourselves to ideas and concepts.

Why we build together

We love each other

We want not only to be together, but to create something together that we find meaningful.

Over the years of working together we learned to understand each other, trust each other's decisions, and rely on each other's strengths.

Adelia works on design and user experience. I focus more on business logic, analytics, strategy, and the technical side. But we don't work in separate silos: we discuss ideas, argue, look for solutions, and share responsibility for the result.

For us Arfa is a natural continuation of what we've long been doing together.

What Arfa does

We develop our own IT products and work on tasks for which we can find a meaningful solution at the intersection of business, analytics, design, and technology.

We're interested in the entire product journey: understanding the problem, grasping future users' needs, designing a solution, building it, and testing it in practice.

We're a small team, so we can work directly, discuss changes quickly, and avoid creating unnecessary processes for their own sake.

It's important to us to understand exactly what we're creating, for whom, and why. And then — to bring it to a working result.

We are Rafael and Adelia. This is our story and our Arfa.
