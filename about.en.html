The beginning

We met back in school. Instead of going separate ways, we chose a common path — entrepreneurship and innovation economics. It felt like the education that would one day help us build our own thing.

Already in first year we noticed technological entrepreneurship programs and student startup competitions. Coursework gradually grew into our own initiatives.

In second year we organised a student research society from scratch and won a 36,000 RUB grant to develop it. Our first experience where it wasn't enough to just propose an idea — we had to organise work, align people, and deliver a result.

First business

It doesn't start with an idea

In third year we launched CyberKids with a friend — our first offline business. The idea was his; he was employed at the time. We split responsibilities: he — curriculum, Adelia — design, me — legal, economics, finance, and business organisation.

In practice it was far less formal. We drove for equipment together, assembled workstations, filed paperwork, solved daily tasks. We opened early so visitors could sign up or print documents. We paid ourselves no salary — money went to rent.

This showed us business from the other side. It's not enough to invent a service and organise its delivery — you need to understand what sustains the project, what costs it carries, and whether the chosen model justifies the effort.

The offline point closed in autumn 2024; legal wind-down finished in February 2025. We didn't continue that model, but took the experience into the next projects.

Pivot to products

We realised you can launch differently

After the first business we started looking at product creation differently. We wanted to launch fast, avoid wasting resources, and validate ideas before serious investment.

For us this isn't just about saving money. It's an approach to tech entrepreneurship: formulate a hypothesis, find a way to test it, get feedback, decide what's next. Not every idea must become a big business. Sometimes it's wiser to pivot in time than to keep investing in something that doesn't meet expectations.

With this understanding we kept working on our own projects and looking for ways to develop them.

Why together

Not just business partners

We met long before the first projects. Our partnership didn't grow from a random business acquaintance. We understand each other well, know each other's strengths, and know how to go from idea to result together.

Each has a professional focus. Adelia works on design and UX. I handle business logic, analytics, strategy, and the technical side. But these aren't rigid boundaries — we discuss decisions together and both participate in product development.

What matters to us isn't just splitting tasks, but understanding why we do them and what result we're aiming for.

Today

Arfa — the next chapter

We build our own IT products and take on tasks where we can combine business thinking, analytics, design, and development. A small team lets us decide fast, test hypotheses, and not overcomplicate processes where it's not needed.

We don't promise every idea becomes a successful product. Instead we try to understand the problem, validate assumptions, and find a solution that makes sense for the user and can work as a business.

Arfa is built on our experience, mutual trust, and the desire to create things we ourselves believe in.

We are Rafael and Adelia. And we keep building our own.
